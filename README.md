# ReProFile
A ProFile interface for the Apple /// rebuilt from scratch.
# Introduction
About a month ago, I had my heart set on completing my Apple /// setup and getting the ridiculously expensive ProFile Hard Disk. The listings around the web seemed to get more expensive as I searched. But I decided to wait and see if one became available for the price I had decided I would spend. lo and behold, on appeared and I grabbed it as quickly as it went up. ProFile aquired, now for the interface card. Unfortunately, my Apple /// did not come with an interface card so I had to search for that as well. What's even worse than the ProFile's prices are the interface cards. They're about the same and sometimes even more than the drive. I found one I placed a bid on and hoped. No dice. With no interface card, no ProFile. After searching around, I noticed that the card itself seemed rather simple. Mostly 74LS chips with some resistor packs. I thought to myself "why not at least try to make one?" So that's what I did. 
# Planning and Design
Thanks to BitSavers and [Asimov](https://mirrors.apple2.org.za/ftp.apple.asimov.net/documentation/apple3/), I managed to locate Schematics, Technical Manuals, and developer notes for the Profile and the associated perepherals. Next step was to figure out resistor (+ resistor packs), capacitor values, and chips. During the research, I noted that there were two revisions made of the interface card. The only difference was the resistor pack values located at A7 and B7. For the older ribbon-style cable, Resistor packs can be either 100ohm or 330ohm. The cable that was bundled with my Profile drive was the shielded (P/N 590-0202) type, which required the 100ohm packs. (See [Profile Level II service manual](https://mirrors.apple2.org.za/ftp.apple.asimov.net/documentation/apple3/Profile_Level_2_Manual.pdf) page 1). All was going well until I encountered a chip labeled '9334'. No match through DigiKey, Mouser, etc. However, some through eBay for much more than I was willing to spend. Looking for a data sheet gave me a Fairchild Semiconductor DM9334N 8-Bit Addressable Latch. Searching for comparable chips gave me a 74LS259N, minor lettering changes to pinouts but overall same function. Great! BOM created and ordered all for the price of ~40$. I went through the schematic and pictures of the cards using Adobe Fresco/Illustrator and my iPad. Mapping out the logic nets and where they connect on the card. I’ve done similar reverse-engineering on other projects so this part wasn’t new. ![IMG_4858](https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/c1fa966b-04db-4a02-b212-22bc284ffe93)
# Revision 0: Wraps of Wire
I’ve always loved the look of wire wrap and the backplanes of older vintage computers. With that, it’s also easier to diagnose signals because the pins are readily available (this became vital later on). Especially since I have no idea if the modern-day replacement LS chips would work. +1 style and usability points I guess. The methodology I followed was: Power/Ground, Resistors (and resistor packs), Card edge, and finally Chip to Chip nets. Power and ground ran on the back of the card. I figured that, in the event of interference, I would try and limit that by some separation. In this case, the PCB.
